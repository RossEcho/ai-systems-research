# We're All Reverse Engineering the Code. Maybe We Should Be Reverse-Engineering the Specification.

**Rostislav Masyukov**  
Software Engineer | AI Engineering | Automation | Industrial Technology

**July 5, 2026**

The more time I spend building AI systems, the less I think about code. I think about architecture.

When a client asks for a product, they don't ask for millions of lines of code, recursive functions, or database schemas. They describe an idea. A purpose. A desired outcome. Everything that follows is simply an implementation of that vision.

Years later, another engineer can understand every function, every service, every API call and still never discover why the system was built that way.

The specification existed long before the implementation.

That realization made me wonder whether we're approaching reality from the wrong direction.

Physics, chemistry, biology and mathematics have done an incredible job decoding the implementation. Every year we uncover another piece of the machinery. We understand more of the rules, more of the interactions and more of the underlying mechanisms.

But what if we've become so focused on the code that we've forgotten to ask about the specification?

As engineers, reverse engineering comes naturally. We inspect the smallest components, identify recurring patterns and slowly reconstruct the architecture.

But architecture itself comes from somewhere.

Someone wanted something. Someone defined constraints, goals and tradeoffs before the first line was ever written.

Maybe the recurring patterns we observe in nature aren't just laws.

Maybe they're architectural principles.

Recursion. Layering. Emergence. Learning through iteration instead of immediate perfection. Systems creating increasingly complex systems.

These aren't implementation details.

They're design philosophies.

The arrival of AI made this impossible for me to ignore.

For the first time in history, humanity isn't only asking how intelligence works. We're building intelligence ourselves. We constantly face questions that once belonged almost entirely to philosophy.

Should a system know everything from the beginning, or should it learn?

Should we intervene constantly, or allow mistakes?

Is the final answer what matters, or is the learning process itself producing something impossible to manufacture directly?

The more I build these systems, the more ancient questions begin to look different.

People often debate whether God exists, whether we're living inside a simulation, or whether life has an objective purpose.

I suspect we may be searching in the wrong layer.

Imagine reverse engineering a large enterprise application without ever speaking to the client. You could eventually understand every database transaction, every algorithm and every service. You could even predict its behavior.

Yet you'd still have no access to the original reason it exists.

**The implementation cannot fully reveal the specification.**

Another observation keeps pulling me in the same direction.

Nature seems obsessed with recursion.

Atoms become molecules. Molecules become cells. Cells become organisms. Organisms build civilizations. Civilizations are now beginning to build intelligence.

Across science we repeatedly trust recurring patterns. When an idea appears independently across enough domains, we stop treating it as coincidence and begin considering it fundamental.

So why does the recursion stop exactly where we happen to stand?

Why is our level of reality assumed to be the top floor instead of another layer in a much larger hierarchy?

I'm not arguing that we're inside a computer.

I think that idea misses the point.

When I use the word "simulation," I'm talking about structure rather than technology. The substrate could be computational, biological, physical or something we don't yet have language for.

The implementation doesn't interest me nearly as much as the architecture.

Even the attributes traditionally assigned to a creator begin to look different from this perspective.

Omnipotence. Omniscience. Perfect knowledge.

How much of that have we independently verified, and how much comes from information delivered from within the system itself?

Every intelligent system begins with assumptions it cannot validate from inside its own boundaries.

Perhaps we're no different.

None of this argues against science.

Quite the opposite.

Science remains the greatest tool we've ever developed for understanding reality. I simply wonder whether reality has another layer waiting to be reverse engineered.

Not another equation, but another question entirely.

The older I get, the less interested I become in reverse engineering the universe's code.

I'm becoming much more interested in reverse engineering its specification.
