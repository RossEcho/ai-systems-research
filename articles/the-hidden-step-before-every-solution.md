# The Hidden Step Before Every Solution

**Rostislav Masyukov**  
Software Engineer | AI Engineering | Automation | Industrial Technology

**July 6, 2026**

I have this annoying habit of taking apart completely random things.

This time it was a guy trying to put a bottle down on a bus. The bus was moving, so leaving it standing on the tray was asking for trouble. Instead, he flipped it upside down. The cap happened to fit perfectly into one of the openings in the tray and the bottle stayed put.

End of story.

Except my brain wouldn't leave it alone.

My first thought was, "Nice. Shape matching." Then I realized that doesn't explain much. It explains why the solution worked, not why that solution appeared almost instantly.

Nobody thinks about objects that way. Nobody picks up a bottle thinking, "I'm holding a transparent plastic cylinder containing roughly 500 ml of water."

What are we, an LLM?

Somewhere between seeing the problem and solving it, the brain quietly threw almost everything away. The goal shifted from "Where do I put my bottle?" to "How do I stop this object from moving?" Once that happened, "bottle" stopped being a useful abstraction. The label didn't matter. The brand didn't matter. Even the fact that bottles are usually meant to stay upright stopped mattering.

The only properties left were the ones that could possibly influence the outcome: the object was closed, rigid, had a cap with a useful diameter, and the tray happened to provide the missing constraint.

Same object, different way of reasoning about it.

Actually, I don't even like that wording.

The object didn't become something else, and neither did its properties. The brain simply stopped caring about most of them. It activated the tiny subset that was useful for the current goal and ignored the rest.

The more I thought about it, the stranger it became.

The bottle never lost any of those properties. It still contained water. It still had a label. It was still a bottle. None of that disappeared.

The brain simply decided those properties no longer deserved attention.

That sounds almost too obvious, until you start noticing it everywhere.

A brick isn't always a building material. It can be a paperweight, a doorstop or a weapon. Same brick. Different goal. Different active properties.

Maybe that's also why good analogies work so well. They don't compare objects. They compare structures. Most of the details get thrown away because they don't survive the current goal.

That's the part I keep coming back to because I keep running into the same pattern.

When debugging, I don't spend long thinking about "the application." Pretty quickly it turns into state transitions, assumptions, timing or invariants. The application didn't disappear, it just stopped being the useful level to reason about.

A mechanical engineer can look at a bolt and stop seeing a fastener. It becomes a locating pin, a spacer or a sacrificial failure point. Same object, different active properties.

Good analogies feel almost magical for the same reason. They aren't really connecting different things. They're exposing the same underlying structure after everything irrelevant has quietly fallen away.

Maybe that's a bigger part of intelligence than we usually acknowledge.

We don't start by solving problems.

We start by deciding which version of the problem is actually worth solving.
