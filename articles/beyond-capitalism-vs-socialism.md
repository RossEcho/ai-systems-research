# Beyond Capitalism vs. Socialism: Designing a Self-Learning Society

**Rostislav Masyukov**  
Software Engineer | AI Engineering | Automation | Industrial Technology

**July 13, 2026**

Political discussions almost always begin with ideology. Capitalism or socialism. Free markets or central planning. Individualism or collectivism.

I think that's asking the wrong question.

Instead of asking which ideology is correct, we should ask: what kind of system is capable of learning?

The Israeli kibbutz is one of the few long-running examples of a collectivist society that functioned for decades. I don't believe its success came solely from socialism. It came from scale. Members knew one another, shared responsibility, and could rely on trust before bureaucracy. As communities grow, that trust is gradually replaced by administration, and many of the advantages disappear.

That observation led me to a different thought.

Perhaps the solution isn't choosing between capitalism and socialism. Perhaps the solution is choosing the right scale for each.

Imagine a country built from autonomous local communities, or "pockets." Each pocket is intentionally small, around twenty thousand people at most, large enough to sustain itself but still small enough that reputation matters. You may not know everyone personally, but everyone knows someone who knows someone. Once a pocket becomes too large, it naturally divides. Several neighboring pockets can then form a cluster, creating another layer of governance without sacrificing local identity.

Inside each pocket, the community is free to organize itself however it chooses. One may operate as a cooperative, another as a traditional market economy, another as something in between. The national government does not dictate the internal economic model. Instead, it provides the constitutional framework that allows different models to coexist.

The nation's responsibilities remain those that genuinely benefit from scale: defense, courts, currency, constitutional rights, national infrastructure, and macroeconomic stability. Everything else is pushed downward to the smallest level capable of making informed decisions.

Infrastructure investment becomes dynamic rather than equal. Every community receives a guaranteed baseline, but additional development follows marginal benefit. A rapidly growing area may receive more investment today. Once it reaches maturity, attention shifts elsewhere. Development becomes a moving cycle rather than permanent favoritism.

Taxation follows the same philosophy. Communities contribute a mandatory base tax, such as a land lease, alongside taxes based on their economic output. The state does not care how wealth is distributed internally, only that each community contributes fairly to the federation.

Failure is not treated as something to hide until collapse. Communities are continuously evaluated through objective indicators. If warning signs appear, they are invited to present their own recovery plan before an independent national tribunal. Intervention is not punishment but stabilization. The goal is to preserve autonomy whenever possible while preventing catastrophic failure.

Freedom of movement remains fundamental. Anyone may leave a community and seek admission elsewhere. Communities may reject applicants only for objective reasons, such as housing capacity or lack of relevant occupations. Those decisions are appealable. If a rejection is found to be unjustified, the applicant is compensated and the community receives governance penalties. Autonomy exists, but so does accountability.

Perhaps the most unusual aspect of the model concerns social welfare.

Today, vulnerable populations are often viewed primarily as financial burdens. This creates incentives to minimize costs rather than maximize human outcomes.

What if the incentives were reversed?

Communities that successfully integrate people with disabilities, chronic illnesses, or other barriers into meaningful lives receive national rewards. Those unable to work, such as many elderly citizens, are still valuable because caring for them creates employment, strengthens social bonds, and contributes measurable social value. National medical and psychological evaluations prevent communities from manipulating classifications while allowing incentives to reflect the true difficulty of each case.

In this system, the weakest members of society are not liabilities. They become opportunities for communities to demonstrate competence and compassion.

Competition also changes.

Communities do not compete solely for wealth. They compete for governance quality, education, health, resilience, and successful social integration. The purpose of competition is not to create winners and losers but to identify better solutions.

When one pocket consistently outperforms others, the question becomes, "What did they discover?" Successful ideas are studied, tested, and recommended as new national best practices. Importantly, they are recommendations rather than mandates. Diversity is preserved because diversity is the engine of innovation. If every community is forced into the same model, the country loses its ability to learn.

This is where the idea begins to resemble cybernetics more than politics.

Each community behaves like an autonomous agent within a larger network. It receives inputs, makes local decisions, produces measurable outcomes, and continuously adapts through feedback. Higher levels of government exist not to micromanage but to maintain stability, coordinate between communities, and update the system as new information emerges.

The objective is not to maximize GDP.

It is not to maximize equality.

It is not even to maximize freedom.

The objective is to maximize the nation's capacity to learn.

The strongest network is rarely the one with the strongest individual node. It is the one in which weak nodes are strengthened before they become points of failure.

Instead of abandoning struggling communities, the system helps optimize them. Their improvement strengthens the entire network.

Perhaps that is the wrong economic model.

Perhaps it is not an economic model at all.

Perhaps it is simply systems engineering applied to society.
